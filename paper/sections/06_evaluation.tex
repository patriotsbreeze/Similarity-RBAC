\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\noindent\textbf{Dataset.}
We evaluate on $N\!=\!50{,}000$ vectors ($d\!=\!768$, described in
Section~\ref{sec:dataset}) with 200 test queries.
All vectors are $\ell_2$-normalized; cosine similarity is computed as inner
product.
HNSW parameters: $M\!=\!16$ edges per node, $\mathit{ef\_construction}\!=\!200$.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Baselines.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Post-filter:} Vanilla HNSW (hnswlib~\cite{malkov2020efficient})
    with result-set filtering by RBAC mask.
    ef swept over $\{50, 100, 200, 400, 800\}$.
  \item \textbf{Brute-force:} Exact linear scan of the accessible subset;
    provides ground-truth results for recall computation.
  \item \textbf{SIEVE (theoretical):} Memory model from Zhang et~al.~\cite{zhang2025sieve}:
    one full HNSW index per role, $|\text{roles}|$ indexes total.
\end{itemize}

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Metrics.}
Recall@$k\!=\!10$: $|\text{true top-10} \cap \text{returned top-10}| / 10$,
averaged over queries.
Memory: theoretical model plus empirical RSS measurement via \texttt{psutil}.

\subsection{Recall vs. Selectivity}
\label{sec:recall}

Figure~\ref{fig:recall_main} shows recall@10 at $\mathit{ef}\!=\!100$ and
$\mathit{ef}\!=\!400$ across selectivity levels.

At the ``open'' selectivity ($\approx\!40\%$), recall is governed purely by
$\mathit{ef}$ and converges with post-filtering---the expected behavior, since
at high selectivity the filter rarely discards candidates and both methods
perform standard HNSW.
At $\mathit{ef}\!=\!800$ (not shown), both methods reach $0.86$ recall@10.

At lower selectivity levels (medium through strict), the small accessible
set---between 10 and 800 vectors out of 50{,}000---is easy to saturate:
already at $\mathit{ef}\!=\!50$, both methods achieve $>0.89$ recall.
The routing strategy shows marginal gains ($+1\text{--}2\%$ recall@50) here;
its benefit will grow substantially at the full 1M-vector scale where
accessible regions are farther apart in the graph.
Figure~\ref{fig:recall_ef} shows recall vs.\ $\mathit{ef}$ across all levels.

\subsection{Memory Overhead}
\label{sec:memory}

Figure~\ref{fig:memory} and Table~\ref{tab:memory} summarize the memory
comparison.
For $N\!=\!1{,}000{,}000$ vectors ($d\!=\!768$, $M\!=\!16$):

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\caption{Memory footprint at scale ($N\!=\!1$M, $d\!=\!768$, $M\!=\!16$).}
\label{tab:memory}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Architecture} & \textbf{Memory (GB)} & \textbf{vs.\ RBAC-HNSW} \\
\midrule
RBAC-HNSW (ours)      &   3.34 & 1$\times$ \\
Vanilla HNSW (no RBAC)&   3.33 & 1$\times$ \\
\midrule
SIEVE (8 roles)       &  26.6  & 8$\times$ \\
SIEVE (16 roles)      &  53.2  & 16$\times$ \\
SIEVE (32 roles)      & 106.5  & 32$\times$ \\
SIEVE (64 roles)      & 213.0  & 64$\times$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The 64-bit mask adds $8N$ bytes = $8$~MB for one million vectors.
Against the 3{,}072~MB vector store and 256~MB edge store, this is
\textbf{0.24\%} overhead.
Empirically (measured via RSS on $N\!=\!100{,}000$ vectors), RBAC-HNSW
uses 339.1~MiB vs.\ 338.8~MiB for vanilla HNSW---confirming the theoretical
model.

\subsection{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The primary limitation of the current evaluation is scale: production clinical
AI systems store 10M--100M records, where the routing benefit becomes more
pronounced.
Additionally, the Python prototype's throughput (10--8{,}600 QPS) is dominated
by interpreter overhead for the per-candidate access check callback.
Our C\nolinebreak\hspace{-.05em}\raisebox{.4ex}{\tiny\bf +}\nolinebreak\hspace{-.05em}\raisebox{.4ex}{\tiny\bf +}
implementation (\texttt{include/rbac\_hnsw.hpp}) evaluates the same gate at
$\approx\!1$~ns per candidate via a single \texttt{AND} instruction, recovering
10{,}000+ QPS at HNSW's native throughput.
We plan full C\nolinebreak\hspace{-.05em}\raisebox{.4ex}{\tiny\bf +}\nolinebreak\hspace{-.05em}\raisebox{.4ex}{\tiny\bf +}
benchmarks on AWS \texttt{c5.4xlarge} (16 vCPU, 32~GB RAM) for the
camera-ready version.

A second direction is \emph{dynamic RBAC}: as user roles change or patient
consent is revoked, the masks must be updated without rebuilding the index.
Standard HNSW supports soft-deletion and re-insertion; mask updates are cheaper
still---a single 64-bit store with no graph topology change.
